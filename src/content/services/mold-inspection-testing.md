A musty smell that won't go away after a leak dried up. A tenant complaining about respiratory symptoms in a unit with no visible stains. A home sale stalled because the buyer's agent wants documentation before closing. These are the situations that call for mold inspection and testing, not a remediation crew, because the first question isn't how to clean it up, it's whether a problem actually exists and how big it is.

## What Mold Inspection and Testing actually involves

Mold inspection and testing is a diagnostic process, separate from remediation work. An AMRT-trained inspector walks the property looking for moisture sources, staining, and conditions that support mold growth, then uses equipment such as moisture meters, thermal imaging, and a borescope to check inside wall cavities or under flooring without demolition. Where visible growth or elevated moisture is found, surface samples (tape lifts or swabs) identify what's growing. Where the concern is airborne spore levels, air sampling pulls a measured volume of air through a cassette for lab analysis, usually compared against an outdoor control sample taken the same day. Most inspections take one to three hours on site depending on square footage and the number of samples collected. Lab turnaround for lifted samples typically runs three to five business days, though rush processing is often available.

## Our process

1. **Interview and visual walkthrough.** We ask about past leaks, humidity complaints, HVAC issues, and recent water events, then inspect visible surfaces, crawlspaces, attics, and areas around plumbing and foundation penetrations.
2. **Moisture mapping.** Moisture meters and thermal imaging identify damp materials that may be feeding hidden growth behind drywall, under flooring, or inside cavities, even where nothing is visible yet.
3. **Sample collection.** Based on what the walkthrough turns up, we collect air samples, surface samples, or both, including an outdoor control sample to give the lab a fair baseline for comparison.
4. **Lab analysis and report.** Samples go to a third-party laboratory for species identification and spore count. We don't run our own lab, which keeps the results independent of whoever ends up doing remediation.
5. **Written findings and recommendations.** You get a report that states what was found, where, at what concentration, and whether remediation is warranted, along with a recommended scope if it is.

## What separates a good mold inspection from a bad one

The most common shortcut is skipping moisture investigation and going straight to air sampling. Air samples tell you what's airborne that day, but if the moisture source behind the wall isn't identified, a clean-looking report can miss an active problem growing two feet away from where the sample was pulled. A thorough inspection always pairs sampling with moisture mapping, because mold doesn't colonize dry materials.

Another gap is relying on a single sample location in a multi-room concern. Spore counts vary by room, by HVAC zone, and by proximity to the moisture source, so one sample in a hallway doesn't characterize a basement or a bathroom with a separate issue. Adjusters reviewing a claim also look for an outdoor control sample and a lab report from an accredited third party, not just a verbal opinion, because that documentation is what supports a remediation scope if the claim moves forward.

## Seasonal & regional considerations

Central Minnesota's humid summer stretch, combined with basements and crawlspaces common in Little Falls' older housing stock, creates a window where condensation on cool foundation walls and slow-draining sump pits can feed growth without an obvious leak. Winter brings the opposite risk: tightly sealed homes running forced-air heat can trap moisture from showers, cooking, and even firewood storage, especially in homes without adequate bathroom or kitchen ventilation. Inspections after spring thaw or late summer humidity peaks tend to catch the conditions that drive most of the calls we get.
