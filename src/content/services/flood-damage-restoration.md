## What Flood Damage Restoration actually involves

Flood damage restoration deals with water that originated outside the home, typically from river overflow, overland surface water, or a sewer system overwhelmed by storm volume. The IICRC classifies this as Category 3 ("black") water, meaning it's presumed contaminated regardless of how clear it looks. That classification changes almost everything about the job: which materials can be dried in place, which have to be cut out and discarded, and what PPE and containment the crew sets up before extraction even starts.

The work usually involves pumping or extracting standing water, removing silt and debris left behind as the water recedes, discarding porous materials that absorbed contaminated water, and then drying the remaining structure with the same engineered airflow and monitoring used on any major water loss. Along the Mississippi River corridor through Morrison County, that often means fieldstone or block foundations, older tile drainage systems, and finished basements where water has wicked up framing and insulation well above the visible waterline.

## Our process

1. **Contamination and safety assessment.** We identify the water category, check for electrical and structural hazards, and determine what PPE and containment the space needs before anyone enters.
2. **Extraction and debris removal.** Pumps and extraction units remove standing water while crews clear silt, sediment, and flood debris that settles into flooring and wall cavities.
3. **Material removal per contamination protocol.** Carpet, pad, drywall, and insulation that contacted floodwater are documented and removed rather than dried, following IICRC S500 category 3 guidance.
4. **Antimicrobial treatment and structural drying.** Remaining framing, subfloor, and masonry are treated and dried with monitored airflow and dehumidification, with moisture readings logged until materials reach dry standard.
5. **Documentation and reconstruction handoff.** Photos, moisture logs, and material disposal records go into the claim file, and the space is prepped for rebuild where materials were removed.

## What separates a good flood response from a bad one

The most common mistake is treating floodwater like a clean-water leak: drying out carpet pad or drywall that should have been discarded, or skipping the antimicrobial step because the water "looked clean" after it receded. That shortcut can leave bacteria and odor-causing residue trapped in porous materials long after the space feels dry to the touch.

A careful crew documents the waterline on every wall, photographs materials before removal, and keeps moisture logs through the full drying cycle, not just the first and last day. Adjusters reviewing a flood claim typically want to see that contaminated materials were removed (not just dried), that drying equipment ran long enough to reach standard on structural moisture content, and that the scope matches what the policy or NFIP claim actually covers.

Little Falls sits on the Mississippi River, and spring is the season that matters most here: snowmelt combined with spring rain raises river levels and pushes groundwater up against older foundations faster than sump systems can keep pace.
