When the drying equipment comes out and the insurance scope is finalized, a homeowner is often left staring at open stud bays, a missing section of subfloor, or a kitchen stripped to the studs after a fire. That's where reconstruction starts: turning a structure that's been stabilized and dried back into a livable, code-compliant space. This isn't patch-and-paint work. It's framing, electrical rough-in, drywall, trim, and finishes handled in the right order so the final result passes inspection and matches what was there before the loss.

## What Reconstruction Services actually involves

Post-damage reconstruction picks up where mitigation leaves off. Once a structure is dry (or demolition from fire or sewage contamination is complete), the rebuild phase covers structural framing repairs, subfloor and underlayment replacement, drywall and insulation, electrical and plumbing rough-in coordination, and finish work like flooring, cabinetry, paint, and trim. For older homes in the Little Falls area, that often means working around balloon-framed walls, plaster-and-lath transitions, or knob-and-tube wiring remnants that need to be addressed before new drywall goes up. Timelines vary widely: a single-room rebuild after a contained pipe burst might run two to three weeks, while a fire damage reconstruction involving structural members and multiple rooms can run several months once permitting and material lead times are factored in.

## Our process

1. **Scope of work development.** We document existing damage, measure affected areas, and write a detailed scope that itemizes materials, labor, and sequencing, cross-referenced against the mitigation report so nothing gets rebuilt over a problem that wasn't actually resolved.
2. **Permits and code review.** Structural repairs, electrical, and plumbing work typically require permits through the local building department. We pull the required permits and build to current code, which sometimes means upgrades beyond what was originally in the house.
3. **Framing and rough-in.** Damaged studs, joists, and subfloor are replaced or sistered in. Electrical, plumbing, and HVAC rough-in is coordinated with licensed trades before insulation and drywall close the walls back up.
4. **Finishing.** Drywall, taping, texture, paint, trim, flooring, and cabinetry are installed to match the pre-loss condition, or to the finish level agreed to in the scope.
5. **Final walkthrough.** We inspect the completed work against the scope and photos, and schedule any required final inspections before calling the job complete.

## What separates a good reconstruction response from a bad one

The most common mistake is starting the rebuild before the structure is verified dry or before contaminated materials are fully removed. Closing up walls over damp framing or soot residue that wasn't properly cleaned leads to callbacks, odor complaints, and sometimes mold growth behind new drywall months later. A second issue is scope drift: contractors who rebuild without a written, itemized scope tend to run into disputes with insurance adjusters over what was actually damaged versus what's being upgraded. Adjusters generally want to see photo documentation tied to specific line items, moisture readings or clearance testing where applicable, and a clear separation between like-kind-and-quality repairs and betterment the homeowner is paying for separately. Good reconstruction work also sequences trades correctly, electrical and plumbing rough-in inspected before insulation goes in, insulation inspected before drywall, so nothing gets hidden that an inspector or adjuster needs to see.

## Seasonal & regional considerations

Central Minnesota's freeze-thaw cycle affects reconstruction timing more than people expect. Framing and exterior work scheduled in deep winter can be delayed by frozen ground conditions or material curing times, and homes with active moisture intrusion need to stay sealed and heated through the rebuild to prevent new freeze damage to exposed framing. Permitting timelines through local building departments can also shift seasonally, since inspectors handle a heavier load during the spring and summer building season.
