## What Commercial Restoration actually involves

Commercial restoration covers the structural drying, soot and odor removal, and rebuild work needed after water intrusion, fire, or storm damage in offices, retail spaces, multi-tenant buildings, and light industrial facilities. The scope differs from a house call in scale and complexity: larger HVAC systems that can carry moisture or soot into spaces far from the source, suspended ceiling grids and plenum spaces that hide saturation, multiple stakeholders (property manager, tenants, insurance carrier) who all need updates, and a harder requirement to keep part of the building operating while the rest is under containment. Equipment is sized accordingly: commercial-grade air movers and LGR dehumidifiers staged by zone, negative air machines for isolating affected areas from active work or retail space, and thermal foggers or ozone treatment for smoke odor that's worked into HVAC ductwork and porous ceiling tile.

## Our process

1. **Loss assessment and business-impact scoping.** We walk the affected space with the property manager or tenant, document what's wet, charred, or contaminated, and flag what can stay in service versus what needs to come offline immediately.
2. **Containment to protect unaffected areas.** Poly barriers and negative air pressure isolate the work zone from retail floors, inventory, or occupied offices so the rest of the business can keep functioning.
3. **Extraction, drying, or soot removal staged by zone.** Rather than treating the building as one job, we work section by section, which lets us hand sections back to the tenant as they clear rather than waiting for the whole building to finish.
4. **Moisture monitoring and documentation for the carrier.** Daily readings, photos, and a written log go into the file your adjuster reviews, dried per IICRC S500 standard practice where water is involved.
5. **Reconstruction and make-ready.** Drywall, ceiling grid, flooring, and paint are scoped and rebuilt to match code and get the space reopened.

## What separates a good commercial restoration response from a bad one

The most common miss is treating a commercial building like an oversized house: drying the visible floor area and ignoring the plenum space above the ceiling tile, where moisture and soot both travel through return air. A thorough response pulls ceiling tiles to check the grid and deck, checks behind millwork and under raised flooring in server rooms or retail counters, and documents moisture readings in writing rather than verbally. Adjusters also look for a clear phase plan: which areas were contained, when they were released back to the tenant, and what readings justified it. Operators who skip documentation or dry only what's visible tend to generate reopened claims when hidden moisture surfaces as mold weeks later.

## Seasonal & regional considerations

Central Minnesota winters bring a specific commercial risk: unoccupied suites or seasonal retail spaces where a frozen supply line or sprinkler head goes undetected over a weekend, and by Monday several hundred square feet of ceiling tile and carpet are saturated. Spring thaw along the Mississippi River corridor raises groundwater and can push moisture into below-grade commercial space and older downtown storefronts with masonry foundations. Humid summer stretches add mold risk for any business storing paper goods, textiles, or food product in a space that sat wet for more than 48 hours.
